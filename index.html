At a glance

How Victoria compares with the other states and territories on the latest figures. Tap a heading to jump to the evidence.

Where Victoria lags

  • ✘Debt: the highest of any state on both main measures, about $61,000 per household (NSW about $39,000). Interest costs $8.9B a year, about $24 million a day, and it is still rising. Victoria owes more than NSW, SA and Tasmania combined.
  • ✘Tax: the highest tax burden, the most property taxes and the lowest land tax threshold. It taxes the most and still borrows the most.
  • ✘Jobs and growth: the highest unemployment of any state, and output per person is falling, which weakens the base that has to repay the debt.
  • ✘Housing: the only large state with falling approvals, and the least social housing in the country.
  • ✘Credit rating: in the lowest S&P tier, so borrowing costs more.
  • ✘Integrity: the largest infrastructure cost overruns, and weaker anti-corruption powers than NSW.

Where Victoria does well

  • ✔Schools: the highest average NAPLAN results of the states.
  • ✔Power prices today: slightly cheaper than other east-coast states (about $6 to $19 a week), but at risk as coal closes.
  • ✔Housing completions: a long record of building more homes per head than NSW.
  • ✔Attracting people: the largest population increase of any state.

The strengths are real but narrow: none of them reduces the debt.

⚠ Warning Not just VictoriaQueensland, South Australia and Tasmania are now adding debt quickly. Queensland's net debt is forecast to nearly double in three years. Victoria starts from the highest base, but the trend is a national one.
The connecting threadHigh debt pushes up interest, which pushes up tax. High property taxes discourage housing investment, weaker supply raises rents and homelessness, and slow growth in output per person leaves each Victorian less able to carry the debt. NSW shows a large services-based state can run lower debt and lower tax at the same time.

⚠ Warning Other states borrowing fast, and what for

Borrowing to build long-lived assets (hospitals, transport) is easier to justify than borrowing to cover day-to-day running costs. South Australia's debt is almost all for building; Tasmania's is mostly running deficits; Queensland's is both. Victoria has done both.

Back where we started, with less to sell

✘ Weaker than 1992

In 1992 Victoria's public sector debt was about a third of GSP. Today NFPS net debt is about 30% of GSP. The ratio is roughly the same, but every other part of the position is weaker than in 1992.

Why it's worse than the ratio suggests

  • ✘The debt is far larger: nearly three times as much after inflation, and about 1.7 times as much per Victorian.
  • ✘It was built in good times: the 1992 debt hit at the bottom of a deep recession with unemployment over 11%. Today's was reached during years of growth and record-low interest rates.
  • ✘The big assets have already been sold or leased, and what remains would cover only about 10 to 20% of today's debt.
  • ✘Nothing stops it happening again: Victoria has no legally binding limit on debt. Its own targets have been reset several times since 2020.

What was the income worth?

$3.3B

A year Victorians now pay the private owners of the five electricity distribution networks (regulator-approved revenue for 2026–31, up from $2.4B a year in 2021–26). A guaranteed return on the owners' capital is built into that charge.

$1.4B

About the yearly return on capital built into those charges: the rate of return makes up about 42% of network revenue. It pays the owners' lenders and shareholders, so it is not all profit, but in public hands it would have been income to the state.

$2–3B

Rough estimate of the interest saved each year in the late 1990s, when about $30B of sale proceeds paid off debt at interest rates of 7–10%.

The balanceat the time, the interest saved was worth more than the income given up, and the state shed the commercial risk of running power stations. But network income grows with the population and is guaranteed by regulation, while the debt saving was a one-off that later governments have since spent. Keeping the monopoly networks and putting their income toward debt would very likely have left Victoria better off today. The interest saving is an estimate; the network figures are from the regulator.

How the regulated return worksThe Australian Energy Regulator caps the return on the money invested in the networks (the "rate of return"), resetting it every five years in line with interest rates: 9.7% in 2011–15, 6.1% in 2016–20 and 4.7% in 2021–26. On top of that, owners recover depreciation and running costs, can earn bonuses for beating cost and reliability targets, and every dollar of approved new capital spending is added to the asset base and earns the same return for decades. So the profit is capped and steady rather than large, which is exactly what makes it a reliable income stream.

Can debt be locked down?

Most debt rules overseas have been bent or broken. The one that has held is written into a constitution and makes governments repay any breach.

Do debt rules actually work? A reality checkOne clear success (Switzerland), several partial ones, and a few that fail by design.Show detail +Hide −
OptionWorks?What happened
Dollar debt ceiling (US federal)NoSet by the same Congress that approves the spending. Raised, extended or redefined 78 times since 1960.
Constitutional debt brake (Switzerland)YesApproved by 85% of voters in 2001. Federal debt fell about CHF 27 billion from 2003 to 2019; overruns must be repaid in later years.
Constitutional debt brake (Germany)PartlyHeld for years, then in March 2025 a two-thirds vote exempted defence spending above 1% of GDP and created a €500 billion infrastructure fund.
Balanced budget rules (US states)PartlyOperating budgets mostly balanced, but some states underfunded pensions instead: debt by another name.
Independent fiscal council (UK, Ireland)Not aloneMakes forecasts more honest; cannot stop borrowing. Victoria already has a Parliamentary Budget Office.
Asset-sale proceeds to debtWrong questionA sale is a one-off that trades permanent income for a single debt cut. Monopoly assets are usually better kept, with their returns applied to debt.
Borrow only for infrastructure ("golden rule")NoVictoria effectively runs this now: an operating surplus while net debt climbs from $175.6B to $199.3B. The UK's version was reinterpreted along the way and abandoned in the 2008 financial crisis.
What would make a rule stick
  1. Hard to change. Victoria already requires a three-fifths majority in both houses to privatise water. A debt limit could be protected the same way, or by referendum.
  2. A ratio on the broadest base. A debt-to-GSP limit on the whole public sector, including private-partnership contracts.
  3. Breaches must be repaid, on a schedule written into law, as Switzerland requires.
  4. Keep income-earning assets and apply their dividends to debt; no sale without a special majority.

The 28 November election: what's on offer

The parties' own policies on debt, services, cost of living and public housing, as announced and reported up to 18 November 2026. This page does not recommend a vote.

Where the race stands

  • •Late polls have the Liberal–National Coalition ahead of Labor; Newspoll put the gap at 10 points two-party preferred.
  • •The Coalition needs about 16 more seats for a majority, so a hung parliament is possible.
  • •One Nation is polling strongly and could hold the balance of power if no party wins a majority.

Your two votes, and what each one does

  • 1Lower house (Legislative Assembly) decides who governs. You vote in your local electorate, but the party that wins most of the 88 seats forms government. This is the vote for the direction of the state, including debt. Number every box in order of preference; a single "1" is informal and won't count.
  • 2Upper house (Legislative Council) decides what passes. It reviews and can block or amend the government's bills, so it acts as the check on whoever wins. Crossbench parties often hold the balance here. New rules this year: number at least 5 boxes above the line (parties) or at least 5 below the line (candidates), and your preferences go only where you put them.
  • 3The two votes can be different. Many voters back the party they want governing in the lower house, then use the upper house to support a check they value, such as parties that insist on costings or on protecting services.
  • 4Where you rank the parties you like least still matters. If your first choice can't win, your vote flows on in the order you set.

The trade-offs in plain terms

  • ⚠Debt vs services: stopping debt growth means spending less; more services and public housing mean spending more or taxing more. No party offers both.
  • ⚠Tax cuts vs debt: every dollar of tax cut has to be found in spending cuts before any debt is repaid.
  • ⚠Even the best case is slow: stopping new borrowing from 2032 would bring debt back to 1999 levels (about 4% of GSP) only around 2070, as the economy grows.

Questions to test any promise

  1. Are the costings published and independently checked before polling day?
  2. Are savings named (projects, staff numbers, dates) or vague?
  3. Is there a dated debt or surplus target you can hold them to?
  4. Is every tax cut matched by a named saving?
  5. What did this party do with debt the last time it governed? (See the chart above.)
